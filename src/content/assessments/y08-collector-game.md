## Four weeks, four parts

- **Week 1, Part 1: Plan.** Talk to your end-user, write their needs, plan your changes and write an algorithm for your own feature. Checkpoint 1 at the end of Week 1.
- **Weeks 2–3, Part 2: Build.** Add your changes one part at a time, with a use for A, new outputs and loops. Checkpoint 2 at the end of Week 2.
- **Week 3, Part 3: Test.** Test every control and the limits, then watch your end-user play. Checkpoint 3 at the end of Week 3.
- **Week 4, Part 4: Showcase.** Make a three-page Google Site with your game on it.

Open the starter game with the button above, play it, then click **Edit Code** to make your own copy. At each checkpoint, click **Share** in MakeCode and post the link privately on the Classroom task.

<div class="rules">

**Keep everyone safe.** Keep your end-user's name, photo and school, and your surname, off your site. No younger child? Ask your kaiako: a classmate can act as your end-user. Home play-tests need an adult there.

**No generative AI.** You must not use it at any stage. That includes Gemini in Google Docs, Sites or Classroom, any AI helper in MakeCode, and AI-made pictures or writing.

## Help with your template

Open each question to see what it's asking.

### The links at the top

Paste your game's share link (from **Share** in MakeCode) and your Google Site's link (after you publish it). Your kaiako looks at your game and site through these links, so check both open.

### My end-user

Your end-user is the younger child you're making the game for. Write who they are to you (like "my cousin"), not their name. Then write their age, the games they like and what they find hard.

### Needs

A need is something your game must do for your end-user, like big sprites or easy controls. Write at least three. Beside each one, write how your game will meet it.

### My changes, in parts

Break your changes into at least three parts, such as hazards, lives and your own feature. Write what each part will do. Building one part at a time makes problems easier to find.

### Algorithm for my own feature

An algorithm is numbered steps, in order, that a computer could follow. Name your feature first. Then write every step, with nothing missing.

### Prediction and a second way

Write what you think will happen on screen when your steps run. Then describe another way your feature could work. Say which way you chose and why it suits your end-user.

### Debugging table

Add a row every time your code gives an error or does something you didn't want. Write what went wrong, why it happened and how you fixed it.

### One loop explained

Pick one loop block you added. Say what it repeats, and what would happen in your game without it.

### Testing table

Before each test, write what you expect to happen. Then do the test and write what really happened. Test every control, the edge of the screen, just below, at and just above your win point, and losing your last life. If something fails, fix it and test again.

### End-user feedback

Watch your end-user play your game. Write what they said and did, and whether the controls were easy for them. Then write at least one change you made because of it.

### File types and Sites features I chose

For each file type and Google Sites feature you used, say why you used it. Then compare two options for one choice, such as PNG or JPG, and explain why yours suits your site better.

### How my game could affect my end-user

Explain one good and one bad way your game could affect your end-user, and a choice you made because of it. Explain how children's games have changed since your parents were young. Then describe a time two needs clashed, such as more levels and short play time, and what you chose.

### Sources and help I used

List every website, video, picture and person that helped you, with a link where you can.
